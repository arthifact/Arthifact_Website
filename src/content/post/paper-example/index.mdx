## Introduction

A measurement gives us part of the picture. Reconstructing the signal between measurements requires a model and a choice about what to keep. Here, we use a small inverse problem to make that choice visible.

The known signal is sampled at 24 positions in $[0,1]$. We add Gaussian noise with standard deviation $0.12$ and fit a Fourier series. With enough coefficients, the fit can follow the measurements closely and still miss the underlying shape.

## Method

Write the signal as a weighted sum of basis functions, $f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{20}c_j\phi_j(x)$. The constant term and ten sine–cosine pairs give us 21 coefficients. The sampling matrix $A$ evaluates each basis function at a measured position.

<div id="eq-observations" />

$$
\mathbf y = A\mathbf c + \boldsymbol\varepsilon,
\qquad \boldsymbol\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2 I).
\tag{1}
$$

We recover the coefficients by balancing the data fit with a penalty on high frequencies:

<div id="eq-objective" />

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{\mathbf c}&=\underset{\mathbf c}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;E(\mathbf c),\\
E(\mathbf c)&=\lVert A\mathbf c-\mathbf y\rVert_2^2
+\lambda\mathbf c^{\mathsf T}R\mathbf c.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
$$

The diagonal entries of $R$ are $k^4$ for frequency $k$; the constant term is unpenalized. Higher frequencies therefore cost more. For $\lambda>0$, the solution satisfies

$$
\left(A^{\mathsf T}A+\lambda R\right)\widehat{\mathbf c}
=A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y.
\tag{3}
$$

The parameter $\lambda$ controls the tradeoff. Too little regularization leaves large oscillations. Too much removes useful detail. [Equation (2)](#eq-objective) and [Figure 1](#fig-reconstruction) describe the same choice in two ways.

## Results

The regularized reconstruction in Figure 1 has a root mean square error of $0.2265$, compared with $1.3724$ for the unregularized fit. These errors are evaluated against the known signal on a dense grid, rather than at the noisy sample positions.[^evaluation]

The lowest error among the four settings in [Table 1](#table-errors) occurs at $\lambda=10^{-3}$. That setting is stronger than the value illustrated in Figure 1. The difference is useful: a smoother reconstruction can improve average error while changing small features.

## Code

The reconstruction is a small linear solve. Use least squares for the unregularized case and solve the penalized system when $\lambda>0$.

```python
import numpy as np

def reconstruct(A, B, samples, frequencies, strength):
    weights = np.r_[0, frequencies**4, frequencies**4]
    penalty = np.diag(weights)

    if strength == 0:
        coefficients = np.linalg.lstsq(A, samples, rcond=None)[0]
    else:
        system = A.T @ A + strength * penalty
        coefficients = np.linalg.solve(system, A.T @ samples)

    return B @ coefficients
```
